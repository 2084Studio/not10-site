Was the Cultural Revolutionreally only ten years long?

1966 to 1976 is the familiar frame. But a political movement that transformed the lives of hundreds of millions of people did not suddenly appear on a single day, nor did everything it created disappear when a date on the calendar changed.

“A chapter long closed?”
?

Today, many people around the world still see the Cultural Revolution as a closed chapter of Chinese history, or as a communist tragedy that has nothing to do with them.

But the problems it revealed do not belong to one ideology, or to one country.

  1. When power is no longer restrained,
  2. when a leader is made into an infallible embodiment of truth,
  3. when political loyalty begins to override facts,
  4. when some people are defined as “non-persons”, unworthy of decent treatment,

any society can, step by step, cross lines it once believed could never be crossed.

Only by understanding how a catastrophe takes shape, and how it operates, can we hope to recognize the next one before it begins.

The bookWHYHOW

The lectures mostly answer when the Cultural Revolution happened and what happened. The book goes further: why did it happen, and how, step by step, did it become possible?

Published in several languages, it hopes to help readers from different cultures ask whether what we live through today, and what we will face tomorrow, is a returning “Cultural Revolution 2.0” — or whether the Cultural Revolution never truly ended.

What the book asks

Not Ten Years is concerned not only with who launched the Cultural Revolution or who suffered under it. It also examines how political power entered language, how language divided people into new categories, how institutions lost their ability to protect those inside them, and how ordinary people tried to survive in a world where the rules themselves could suddenly change.

History does not take place only inside Zhongnanhai.

It unfolds in schools, factories, homes and streets — and sometimes on the front door of an ordinary person. It unfolds in schoolsin factoriesin homesin streetsat an ordinary front door
